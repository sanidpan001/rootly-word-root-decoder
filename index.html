The complete guide to English word roots: decode any word like a linguist

Every English word has a biography. Transport is not just seven letters you memorized in school — it is trans (Latin, "across") fused to port (Latin, "carry"), a two-thousand-year-old instruction that still reads perfectly today: carry across. Once you see words this way, vocabulary stops being a list to memorize and becomes a system to decode. That is the entire idea behind Rootly, the free word root decoder at the top of this page: type any English word and it splits the word into its morphemes — prefixes, roots and suffixes — showing you the meaning and origin of every piece, color-coded by language family.

Why does this matter? Because English is a magpie language. Roughly sixty percent of its vocabulary comes from Latin, much of it arriving through French after 1066, and another large share comes from Greek, mostly through scientific and academic coinages of the last four centuries. The remaining core — the short, everyday words like water, house, love, strong — is Germanic, inherited from Old English. A single long English word is often a sandwich of these layers: television joins the Greek tele ("far") to the Latin vis ("see"). When you learn the roots, you are not learning one word; you are learning the key to dozens.

How a word root decoder works

A word root decoder performs morphological analysis, the same first step a linguist takes with an unfamiliar word. It works in three passes. First it peels off prefixes, the syllables at the front that set direction or intensity: re- (again), pre- (before), trans- (across), anti- (against). Then it peels off suffixes, the endings that usually change a word's grammar — -ation turns a verb into "the act of", -able means "capable of", -ology means "the study of". What remains is the root, the semantic core that carries the main meaning. Rootly checks that core against a built-in database of 285 roots, tries the longest match first, and even handles compounds like photograph (photo, light + graph, write) and connecting vowels like the o in speedometer.

Each morpheme is color-coded by origin so the word's history is visible at a glance: violet for Greek, amber for Latin, and teal for Germanic. Hover-free and instant, the breakdown appears the moment you press Decode, together with a confidence note. If a word has no identifiable classical root — perfectly normal for Germanic compounds like replay or everyday words like hello — Rootly says so honestly instead of inventing a fake etymology. A decoder you can trust is one that knows when to stay quiet.

Inside the english root words list: 285 roots, three language families

The heart of Rootly is its english root words list, a hand-compiled word root dictionary of 285 entries: 142 Latin roots, 98 Greek roots and 44 Germanic roots, backed by 92 prefixes and 78 suffixes. Every entry carries four things: the canonical root spelling, its plain-English meaning, its spelling variants (because dict, dic and diction are the same root wearing different clothes), and a word-family list showing the English words grown from it.

The Latin section covers the workhorses of academic English. Port (carry) gives you transport, portable, import, export, deport, report and support. Dict (say) gives you dictate, predict, verdict, contradict and dictionary. Scrib/script (write) gives you describe, manuscript, prescribe and inscription. Vers/vert (turn) quietly powers reverse, convert, invert, divert and even anniversary (the turning of the year). Learn one Latin root and you typically unlock six to ten words at once; learn fifty and several hundred words become transparent.

The Greek section is the language of science, medicine and philosophy. Cardi (heart) sits inside cardiac, cardiology and tachycardia. Derm (skin) explains dermatology, epidermis and hypodermic (literally "under the skin"). Chron (time) connects chronic, chronicle, synchronize and anachronism ("against time" — something in the wrong era). Morph (form) links metamorphosis, amorphous and anthropomorphic. Greek roots love to combine with each other, which is why long scientific terms that look terrifying — electroencephalogram, anyone — are usually just three or four friendly roots holding hands.

The Germanic section is easy to overlook and impossible to do without. These are the oldest roots in the language: kin (family), craft (skill), wright (worker — surviving in playwright and wheelwright), water, sun, moon, star, day, night, friend, love, life, death, heart, head, eye, blood, bone, gold, wood, stone. They rarely appear in textbooks of "root words" because they feel too ordinary, but they explain thousands of compounds: waterfall, sunburn, moonlight, stardust, daybreak, nightmare, friendly, lovely, lively, deadly, hearty, headache, eyewitness, bloodhound, backbone, golden, wooden, stonewall. A decoder that ignored Germanic roots would go silent on the most human part of English.

Word-family trees: one root, a whole vocabulary

Below every decoded word, Rootly grows a word-family tree for each root it found. Decode transport and you get the port tree: portable, import, export, deport, report, support, porter. This is deliberately designed around how memory works. Psychologists call it elaborative encoding — a word remembered inside a network of related words sticks far better than a word memorized alone. When you see that import is "carry in", export is "carry out", deport is "carry away" and report is "carry back" (news), the whole family becomes one logical idea instead of four separate dictionary entries.

Family trees also reveal false friends and true ones. Cred (believe) connects credit, credible, credential and creed — and suddenly incredible ("not believable") and accredit ("to give belief to", i.e. certify) make sense. Mort (death) connects mortal, immortal, mortuary and, surprisingly, mortgage — literally a "death pledge", a loan that dies when it is paid off. These little stories are the reason root-based learning beats flashcards: every word arrives with a narrative attached.

How to study word roots so they stick

A decoder is a tool, not a teacher, so here is the study method that makes roots permanent. First, decode before you look up. When you meet an unknown word, split it yourself — prefix, root, suffix — and guess the meaning from the parts. Then check a dictionary. The small effort of guessing roughly doubles retention compared to reading the definition cold. Second, learn roots in families, not alone. Never learn port by itself; learn it inside transport, import, export, support. Rootly's family trees are built for exactly this. Third, say the story out loud. "Democracy is the people ruling" is stickier than "dem = people, cracy = rule". Fourth, hunt roots in the wild. For one week, try to spot three roots a day in news headlines, subtitles or textbooks — pandemic in a health article, photosynthesis in a biology chapter, benevolent in a novel. Each sighting is a free repetition.

Common pitfalls are worth knowing. Not every similar-looking chunk is a root. The ham in hamster is not the ham of hamlet, and folk etymologies are everywhere — which is why Rootly refuses to guess when it finds no real root. Meanings drift. Sophomore is literally "wise fool" (soph + Greek moros, foolish), a joke from the 1600s about second-year students who think they know everything. The root gives you the history; usage gives you the current meaning; you need both. Finally, spelling changes disguise roots. Cred appears as creed, vis appears as view, fac appears as fect, fect and fic. Rootly's variant matching handles these automatically, and noticing the pattern yourself is one of the great pleasures of the method.

What is the difference between a prefix, a root and a suffix?

A root carries the core meaning of a word (port = carry). A prefix attaches to the front and modifies direction or intensity (trans- = across). A suffix attaches to the end and usually changes the word's grammar or shades its meaning (-ation = the act of).
